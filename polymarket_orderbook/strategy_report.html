Quoting the MLB Moneyline

A two-sided passive market-making strategy on Polymarket MLB moneyline binaries. It is the only positive-expectancy strategy that survived a full search of the strategy space — and it is small, capacity-limited, and gated on one unverified number.

The one-paragraph version

Quote both sides of the moneyline continuously, capture the spread, and retire inventory on a clock before the game resolves. Every taker strategy tested lost roughly the spread at every latency; this one wins because Polymarket charges makers nothing and adverse selection consumes only about a third of the half-spread you capture. The edge is real and it replicated across four independent slates. It is also capacity-limited to roughly $30,000 of deployed capital, above which returns fall, and its headline number rests on an assumed 10% fill rate that this dataset cannot verify.

The premise to discard: this is not a strategy to wait for more capital to run. It saturates at ~$30k and earns less at $240k than at $6k. Surplus capital belongs in a different strategy.

Capacity, not capital, is the constraint

Quote size and inventory cap scaled together, all 34 games, full inventory-and-settlement simulation.

The specification

Walk-forward validated
market
Moneyline only. 1.50¢ mean spread. Totals (10.53¢) and run-line spreads (9.13¢) look richer per share but yielded only 373 and 516 clean fills — too thin to validate.
side
One token per market. YES and NO are the same book mirrored (bid_YES + ask_NO = 100¢, identical size, in 100.0000% of 884,686 simultaneous states). Quoting both is one position, not two.
quote size
500 shares for maximum total PnL; 250 for the best return on capital (+1.87¢/share vs +1.71¢).
inventory cap
±4,000 shares. Counter-intuitively, tight caps hurt — they stop you quoting the very side that would mean-revert the position. A ±250 cap turned +$1,820 into −$277.
quote skew
1.0 spread per unit of inventory/cap. Skew of 2.0 or more lost money in every configuration tested: quoting away from the touch costs more in missed fills than it saves in inventory.
flatten
Taper the cap to zero over the final 40% of the game, quoting only the inventory-reducing side. The single most valuable rule in the spec.
exit
Maker only, never cross. A taker exit costs −1.85¢/share and lost in 41 of 41 games at any fee rate.
fees
Zero on both legs. Polymarket's schedule is takerOnly: true, confirmed by all 16,305 captured trades reporting fee_rate_bps = 0. A fee only ever appears if you cross out.

Why flattening matters more than anything else

A binary contract walks to its outcome. A symmetric quoter therefore buys as the loser falls and sells as the winner rises, and is systematically left holding the wrong side at resolution — where the position pays 0 or 1, not the mid. Before the taper, every worst-case token in the simulation was short a contract that resolved at 100¢.

The two tape sources differ by 0.083¢ in the predicted direction: second-resolution timestamps blur the fill moment and average away part of the immediate adverse move, overstating edge by about 10%. Treat +0.811¢ as the honest per-fill figure. That the bias appeared in the right direction and the right magnitude is itself a check on the measurement.

Adverse selection Measured

On the millisecond tape, a maker captures a +1.068¢ half-spread and gives back +0.293¢ to adverse drift over one second — about 27%, rising to ~65% at ten seconds. The reason it is not worse: roughly 2,000 shares are cancelled for every share traded on this venue, so most fills are liquidity-motivated rather than informed.

Full simulation 53% hit rate

With inventory, latency and settlement modelled: +$1,820 across 34 games, +1.706¢/share. But only 36 of 68 tokens were profitable — essentially a coin flip, with PnL coming from winners being larger than losers. A market-making book should show many small wins and rare large losses; this shows the opposite, which suggests the result is driven more by inventory outcomes than by systematic spread capture. This is the weakest link in the evidence chain.

Data integrity Audited

  • Crossed books (ask ≤ bid) — 0.07% of rows, removed. They fabricate risk-free arbitrage, and any filter written spread <= X admits them, since a crossed book has negative spread.
  • Feed continuity — 112 gap rows in 24.2M (0.0005%). The recording is effectively continuous; no reconnect contamination.
  • Excluded: the 2026-08-29 slate, whose recorder stopped before most games started (5 of 15 games have in-game data, median 41% coverage).

The three risks that actually matter

  • Settlement risk is the tail. Inventory carried into resolution pays 0 or 1, not the mid. It is unbounded by any spread and is the only way this strategy loses badly. The 40% taper exists solely to retire it.
  • Fill clustering compounds inventory. In the jumpiest quintile of order flow, fills arrive a median 3.0 seconds apart versus 38.7s in the calmest. Positions build fastest exactly when price is running — correlated bets, not independent ones. Cap inventory hardest there.
  • Spread compression under your own participation. The 1.50¢ moneyline spread is wide because few compete. The tick is 0.001, so anyone can step ahead of you for a tenth of a cent. Size expectations to a spread that narrows once you are in it.

Hard limits to run with

  • Never cross to exit; work inventory out passively or let the taper do it.
  • Stop-loss on terminal inventory, not on mark-to-market — the mid can look fine while the settlement exposure is the whole position.
  • Kill the quoter if the feed gaps: a stale book is where fabricated edge comes from.

Open questions

Two of these must be resolved before capital is committed.

Fill rate Unverified — blocking

Every figure in this report assumes you win 10% of moneyline flow. That number is an assumption, not a measurement, and it cannot be measured from this data: cancellations outnumber trades ~2,000:1 and cancel queue positions are unobservable, so no queue model is defensible here. The outcome spans an order of magnitude — $38/slate at 2% capture, $668 at 25%. Only resting real orders settles it.

The latency inversion Unexplained — blocking

Faster execution made the simulation worse: $848 at 20 ms versus $1,820 at 500 ms. This contradicts the per-fill analysis, which showed capture collapsing from +1.09¢ to +0.22¢ as latency rose. The likely mechanism is that lower latency wins more fills and the marginal fills lose money — which would mean the profit is not coming from spread capture at all. Do not spend on co-located infrastructure until this is understood.

Jump-intensity sizing Contradictory

Per-fill analysis said scale size down when Hawkes intensity is high (mean IR 16.65 vs 12.69, better in 32/37 games, on 36% of the capital). The full simulation reversed it ($1,199 vs $1,820). The simulation carries the realistic constraints, so trust it — but the disagreement is unresolved and is probably the same phenomenon as the latency inversion.

Deployment sequence

In order. Each step gates the next.

  1. Resolve the latency inversion. Until faster execution stops making things worse, the mechanism behind the profit is not understood and the spec above rests on it.
  2. Run a minimum-size live pilot — rest 5-share orders, log every fill. Measure two numbers: what fraction of flow you win, and what fraction of inventory you unwind passively. Both are unobtainable from historical data.
  3. Size to the pilot's fill rate, not to this report's 10% assumption. Below ~5% capture the strategy is not worth operating.
  4. Deploy at $15k–$30k and no more. Return on capital is best near $15k; total profit peaks near $30k; both decline after.
  5. Keep recording with the millisecond tape. Only 15 of 37 validated games carry it, and it is the only source that measures adverse selection correctly.
Rough season scale, if the 10% assumption holds: ~$400 per slate, ~160 slates in an MLB season, on $30k deployed. Treat that as the optimistic branch of a distribution whose downside is "not worth running", not as a forecast.
